In his paper, Goldsmith describes a possible extension to bivariate data.  
He concatenates data and spline coefficient matrices.  
Specifically, let \(Y(t) = \left[Y_1(t),Y_2(t)\right]\) and let us express this in matrix form as \(Y = [Y_1,Y_2]\), 
where \(Y_i, i\in(1,2)\) corresponds to functional outcomes in the X and Y dimensions respectively concatenated together by 
column, \(B_W^T = [B_{1,W}^T, B_{2,W}^T]\), corresponds to the fixed effect spline coefficients in two dimensions, 
and \(B_Z^T = [B_{1,Z}^T, B_{2,Z}^T]\) corresponds to the random effect spline coefficients in two dimensions.  
Taking advantage of Kronecker product structure\footnote{See appendix},  Goldsmith writes out the following model:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        Y = ZB_Z^T\left(I_2 \otimes\Theta^T\right) + \epsilon\\
        \epsilon\sim N[0, \Sigma\otimes I_n]\\
        \Sigma \sim IW[v, \Psi]\\
        B_{Z,i} \sim N[B_w \boldsymbol{w}_i^T, \text{diag}\left(\sigma_{1,Z}^2, \sigma_{2,Z}^2\right)\otimes P^{-1}]\quad \text{for} \quad i = 1,\dots, I\\
        \sigma_{m,Z}^2 \sim IG[a_{m,Z}, b_{m,Z}]\quad \text{for} \quad m = 1,2\\
        B_{W,i} \sim N[0, \text{diag}\left(\sigma_{1,W_{k}}^2, \sigma_{2,W_{k}}^2\right)\otimes P^{-1}]\\
        \sigma_{m,W_{k}}^2 \sim IG[a_{m,W_{k]}}, b_{m,W_{k}}]\quad \text{for} \quad m = 1,2 \quad\text{and} \quad k = 1,\dots, p.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

Note how fixed and random effects in each dimension are assumed independent.  
This bivariate model allows for modeling position curves in two dimensions concurrently.  
This bivariate model explicitly models correlation between the X and Y position curves through the level 1 residual 
covariance matrix; the X and Y dimensions are independent in both the fixed and random effects.      

Goldsmith remarks \say{Similar extensions to three or more curves \ldots proceed similarly}.  
We continue to follow Goldsmith's lead, giving the following model for three dimensional functional data:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        Y = ZB_Z^T\left(I_3 \otimes\Theta^T\right) + \epsilon\\
        \epsilon\sim N[0, \Sigma\otimes I_n]\\
        \Sigma \sim IW[v, \Psi]\\
        B_{Z,i} \sim N[B_w \boldsymbol{w}_i^T, \text{diag}\left(\sigma_{1,Z}^2, \sigma_{2,Z}^2, \sigma_{2,Z}^3\right)\otimes P^{-1}]\quad \text{for} \quad i = 1,\dots, I\\
        \sigma_{m,Z}^2 \sim IG[a_{m,Z}, b_{m,Z}]\quad \text{for} \quad m = 1,2, 3\\
        B_{W,i} \sim N[0, \text{diag}\left(\sigma_{1,W_{k}}^2, \sigma_{2,W_{k}}^2, \sigma_{3,W_{k}}^2\right)\otimes P^{-1}]\\
        \sigma_{m,W_{k}}^2 \sim IG[a_{m,W_{k]}}, b_{m,W_{k}}]\quad \text{for} \quad m = 1,2, 3 \quad\text{and} \quad k = 1,\dots, p
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

As with the bivariate model, fixed and random effects in each dimension are assumed to be independent.  
This independence assumption is important for two main reasons, one conceptual and another computational.  
First, assuming independence simplifes the covariance term in \(B_{Z,i}\) and \(B_{W,i}\).  
Without this independence assumption, we would need to model the correlation between X and Y fixed and random effect spline 
coefficients\footnote{We do this in the following model.}.  Secondly, assuming independence gives clear intution to the level 1 
residual covariance matrix:  it describes the manner in which the X and Y trajectories vary, after accounting for 
fixed and random effects\footnote{I think technically this would be the cross-variance.}. This trivariate model will serve 
as our first attempt at a trivariate model.  

\subsection{Concerns and Resulting Alternative Model}
Goldsmith's 2016 paper heavily motivates this work.  In his model (and its two dimensional extension), 
he places an inverse-Wishart prior on the level 1 covariance structure.  There is one cause for concern with this: 
for observations in three dimensions with each dimension being observed across a grid of \(D\), the level 1 covariance matrix 
would have to be \(3*D\times 3*D\).  In the case of \(D=100\), that would mean that our level 1 covariance matrix would be 
\(300\times300\).  If the level 1 covariance matrix is modeled as inverse Wishart, then as the dimension of this matrix increases, 
the degrees of freedom of the inverse Wishart also must increase for the distribution to remain proper. 
A result of this is that the Inverse Wishart \(IW\left(n\boldsymbol{S} + \Psi, n + v\right)\) posterior distribution tends to 
concentrate more tightly around its prior expected value: \(\Psi/{\left(v-p-1\right)}\)\cite{zhang_note_2021}. When the scale matrix is 
the identity matrix, this expected value is proportional to the identity matrix itself; using an Inverse Wishart prior with an identity 
scale matrix can lead to over-shrinkage of the estimated covariance matrix towards the identity matrix.

We propose a modified model that allows for correlation among the functional outcomes without using the inverse Wishart distribution: 

\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    Y = ZB_Z^T\left(I_3 \otimes\Theta^T\right) + \epsilon\\
    \epsilon\sim N[0, \sigma_{0}^2 I_n]\\
    B_{Z,i} \sim N[B_w \boldsymbol{w}_i^T, \Omega_Z\otimes P^{-1}]\quad \text{for} \quad i = 1,\dots, I\\
    \Omega_Z \sim IW [v_z, \Psi_z]\\
    B_{W,i} \sim N[0, \Omega_{W,k}\otimes P^{-1}]\\
    \Omega_{W,k} \sim IW[v_{w,k}\Psi_{w,k}]\quad\text{for} \quad k = 1,\dots, p
    \end{split}
    \label{model_centered_iid}
\end{equation}

We have moved the dependence from the level 1 error structure into the fixed and random effects.  
The intuition behind this is that now, rather than a \(300\times 300\) covariance matrix, our model only has 
\(p+1\)\footnote{p matrices for the fixed effects, plus 1 for the random effects.} \(3\times 3\), covariance matrices, s
implifying sampling with STAN.  Stan is a statistical software available in multiple programming languages that allows for easy
 HMC modeling.  With STAN, one can create a model by specifying probability distributions, relationships between parameters, and 
 hierarchical structures (if needed).  There is at least one other instance of Bayesian functional data analysis being performed 
 using STAN, indeed by Goldsmith himself the prior year\cite{goldsmith_generalized_2015}.

In doing this, issues were encountered regarding the physical geometry of the this model in Stan.  
Take for instance the univeriate model in equation \ref{mod1_var}.  Specifically the conditional prior distribution on \(B_{Z,i}\):
\[
B_{Z,i} \mid \sigma_Z^2 \sim N(B_W \boldsymbol{w}_i^T, \sigma_Z^2 P^{-1}),
\]
which written out is:
\[
p(B_{Z,i} \mid \sigma_Z^2) \propto |\sigma_Z^2 P^{-1}|^{-1/2}
\exp\left[
-\frac{1}{2}(B_{Z,i}-B_W \boldsymbol{w}_i^T)'(\sigma_Z^2 P^{-1})^{-1}(B_{Z,i}-B_W \boldsymbol{w}_i^T)
\right].
\]

Because $(\sigma_Z^2 P^{-1})^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{\sigma_Z^2}P$ and $|\sigma_Z^2 P^{-1}| = (\sigma_Z^2)^{K_t}|P|^{-1}$, this simplifies to
\[
p(B_{Z,i} \mid \sigma_Z^2)
\propto
(\sigma_Z^2)^{-K_t/2}
\exp\left[
-\frac{1}{2\sigma_Z^2}
(B_{Z,i}-B_W \boldsymbol{w}_i^T)'P(B_{Z,i}-B_W \boldsymbol{w}_i^T)
\right].
\]

Combining this with a prior on $\sigma_Z^2$ and omitting constants gives the joint density
\[
p(B_{Z,i}, \sigma_Z^2)
\propto
(\sigma_Z^2)^{-K_t/2}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2\sigma_Z^2}
(B_{Z,i}-B_W \boldsymbol{w}_i^T)'P(B_{Z,i}-B_W \boldsymbol{w}_i^T)
\right]p(\sigma_Z^2),
\]
and thus the log-density
\[
\log p(B_{Z,i}, \sigma_Z^2)\propto -\frac{1}{2\sigma_Z^2}
(B_{Z,i}-B_W \boldsymbol{w}_i^T)'P(B_{Z,i}-B_W \boldsymbol{w}_i^T)
-\frac{K_t}{2}\log\sigma_Z^2.
\]

This form reveals a strong coupling between $B_{Z,i}$ and $\sigma_Z^2$ in the joint posterior: when $\sigma_Z^2$ is small, 
the quadratic term must also be small for the density to remain finite, forcing $B_{Z,i}$ to lie close to its 
mean $B_W \boldsymbol{w}_i^T$.  In short, small $\sigma_Z^2$ implies $B_{Z,i} \approx B_W \boldsymbol{w}_i^T$, 
while large $\sigma_Z^2$ allows broader variation.  Geometrically, the high-probability region of $(B_{Z,i},\sigma_Z)$ 
resembles a funnel: flat and wide for large $\sigma_Z^2$, but sharply tapering to 0 as $\sigma_Z^2 \to 0$.

To fix this, the common tool of un-centering is used.  Previous works have made the observation that un-centering can 
improve performance of Hamiltonian Monte Carlo\cite{betancourt_hamiltonian_2013}, and that some models perform better un-centered 
than centered\cite{papaspiliopoulos_general_2007}.  The prior for \(B_{Z,i}\) can be written as:
\[
B_{Z,i} = B_W \boldsymbol{w}_i^T + \sigma_Z P^{-1/2} z_i, \qquad z_i \sim N(\mathbf{0}, I),
\]
which defines a linear transformation
\[
B_{Z,i} = B_W \boldsymbol{w}_i^T + L z_i,\qquad L = \sigma_Z P^{-1/2}.
\]

Solving for \(z_i\):
\[
z_i = L^{-1}(B_{Z,i} - B_W \boldsymbol{w}_i^T = \frac{1}{\sigma_Z} P^{1/2} (B_{Z,i} - B_W\boldsymbol{w}_i^T).
\]
This is transformation of variables.  The Jacobian determinant of this transformation (for fixed $\sigma_Z$) is
\[
\left|\frac{\partial B_{Z,i}}{\partial z_i}\right| = \sigma_Z^{K_t} |P^{-1/2}|.
\]
Substituting into the centered joint density and applying the change of variables gives
\[
\begin{split}
(B_{Z,i}-B_W \boldsymbol{w}_i^T)'P(B_{Z,i}-B_W \boldsymbol{w}_i^T)
&= (\sigma_Z z_i' P^{-1/2})P(P^{-1/2} \sigma_Z z_i)
= \sigma_Z^2 z_i' z_i = \sigma_Z^2 \|z_i\|^2.
\end{split}
\]
The exponential term thus becomes $\exp[-\tfrac{1}{2}\|z_i\|^2]$, and the Jacobian cancels the $(\sigma_Z^2)^{-K_t/2}$ factor exactly:
\[
(\sigma_Z^2)^{-K_t/2}\times\left|\frac{\partial B_{Z,i}}{\partial z_i}\right|
= (\sigma_Z^2)^{-K_t/2} \sigma_Z^{K_t} = 1.
\]
The transformed joint density is therefore
\[
p(z_i, \sigma_Z^2) \propto \exp\!\left[-\tfrac{1}{2}\|z_i\|^2\right] p(\sigma_Z^2),
\]
and taking logs gives
\[
\log p(z_i,\sigma_Z^2) \propto -\tfrac{1}{2}\|z_i\|^2 + \log p(\sigma_Z^2) + \text{const.}
\]

This reparameterization eliminates the multiplicative dependence between $z_i$ and $\sigma_Z^2$.  
To see the difference, we can consider the gradient of both the centered and uncentered log joint distributions of 
\((B_{Z,i},\sigma_Z^2)\):

For the centered parameterization, we can consider the log joint as:

\[
\log p(B_{Z,i}, \sigma_Z^2)\propto -\frac{1}{2\sigma_Z^2}
(B_{Z,i}-B_W \boldsymbol{w}_i^T)'P(B_{Z,i}-B_W \boldsymbol{w}_i^T)
-\frac{K_t}{2}\log\sigma_Z^2,
\]
and we can consider the two gradients as:
\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial B_{Z,i}}\text{log } p(B_{Z,i},\sigma_Z^2) \propto -\frac{1}{2\sigma_Z^2}(B_{Z,i}-B_W \boldsymbol{w}_i^T)
\]
and
\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial \sigma_Z^2}\text{log } p(B_{Z,i},\sigma_Z^2) \propto -\frac{1}{2(\sigma_Z^2)^2}
(B_{Z,i}-B_W \boldsymbol{w}_i^T)'P(B_{Z,i}-B_W \boldsymbol{w}_i^T) - \frac{Kt}{2\sigma_Z^2} + \frac{\partial}{\partial \sigma_Z^2}\text{log }\sigma_Z^2.
\]
In short, the gradients depend on each other.  Compare this, for example, to the uncentered parameterization, with log joint:

\[
\log p(z_i,\sigma_Z^2) \propto -\tfrac{1}{2}\|z_i\|^2 + \log p(\sigma_Z^2).
\]

The two gradients are:
\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial z_i}\log p(z_i,\sigma_Z^2) \propto -z_i\quad \text{and}\quad \frac{\partial}{\partial \sigma_Z^2}\log p(z_i,\sigma_Z^2) \propto \frac{1}{\sigma_Z^2}
\]

In this parameterization, the gradients do not depend on each other; they separate nicely.  
Since HMC relies on the gradients of parameters to update itself, this improves sampling performance.

There were more computational improvements done to this model, some of which are only feasible in the Stan framework.  
First, the large matrix \(Z\) was a dummy matrix, identifying which subject's random effects we're considering.  
This was replaced with a simple integer array.  This speeds things up computationally as we no longer have to multiple a 
\(n\times I\) matrix in the likelihood step.  This portion can probably be done in the Gibbs sampler.  

Second, instead of placing priors specifically on the inverse-Wishart components of the model, we decompose them into 
correlation matrices and scale arrays; this is mathematically equivalent to a prior on \(\Omega\) but is numerically much more stable.  
Impossible is a very presumptive word and rarely appropriate, however doing this in a Gibbs sampler would be much more difficult.  
The Gibbs sampler relies on the inverse-Wishart for its conditionals, and I do not know of any closed form for the Stan decomposition 
in this framework.  It is not immediately apparent that is is conjugate, and would most likely require a Metropolis step within Gibbs.

Thirdly, the priors on the spline coefficients are\(B_{Z,i}\sim N\left(B_{Z,i}-B_W\boldsymbol{\omega}_i^T,\Omega_Z\otimes P^-1\right)\) 
and \(B_{W,k}\sim N\left(0,\Omega_{W,k}\otimes P^{-1}\right)\), respectively.  We compute these covariance terms once per iteration, 
then decompose them via a Cholesky decomposition.  Again, the same issues raised in the preceding paragraph are still present here.  
In short, the easiest way to do take advantage of these computational speed ups is to use Stan.  

So how does this change the form of the model?  It gives us the following model:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    Y = B_{Z,i}^T\left(I_3 \otimes\Theta^T\right) + \epsilon\\
    \epsilon\sim N[0, \sigma_{0}^2 I_n]\\
    B_{Z,i} = B_w\boldsymbol{w}_i^T + L_{Z}z_{Z,i}\quad z_{Z,i} \sim N\left(0,\mathcal{I} \right)\quad\text{for} \quad i = 1,\dots, I\\
    B_{W,k} = L_{W,k}Z_{W,k}\quad Z_{W,k}\sim N\left(0,\mathcal{I}\right)\quad \text{for} \quad k = 1,\dots, p\\
    L_Z^TL_Z = \Omega_z\otimes P^{-1}\\
    L_{W,k}^TL_{W,k} = \Omega_{W,k}\otimes P^{-1}\\
    \Omega_{Z} = D_ZR_ZD_Z^T\quad D_Z = \text{diag}(\tau_z)\quad R_Z \sim JKL(.)\quad \tau_z\sim N^{+}(0,1)\\
    \Omega_{W,k} = D_{W,k}R_{W,k}D_{W,k}^T\quad D_{W,k} = \text{diag}(\tau_{W,k})\quad R_{W,k}\sim JKL(.)\quad \tau_{W,k}\sim N^{+}(0,1)\\
    \end{split}
    \label{model:uncentered}
\end{equation}

This is mathematically equivalent to the model found in \eqref{model_centered_iid}, simply decomposed.
